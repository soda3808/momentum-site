How to Track Expenses in Notion

Notion cannot connect to your bank, and that turns out to be the whole design problem. This walks through a manual expense tracker on the free plan (one database, one formula, two views), then the four places manual tracking falls apart, because that is what decides whether you are still using it in March.

Start by admitting what you are building

You are not building accounting software. Notion has no bank feed, no reconciliation, no double entry. Anyone promising a Notion setup that replaces your banking app is selling you a screenshot.

What Notion is genuinely good at is the thing banking apps are bad at: answering “where did it actually go?” in categories you chose yourself, sitting next to your tasks and notes instead of in a separate app you open once a month and close.

So the target is modest: a log you are still typing into in eight weeks. Every decision below is made in service of that, One of them, how many categories you pick, matters more than the formulas do.

One database or two?

Unlike a habit tracker, where two databases are non-negotiable, expenses work fine in one. A single Transactions table holds both income and spending, and a Type property tells them apart.

Resist splitting income and expenses into separate databases. The moment you do, every “what did I actually net this month?” question needs a rollup across two tables, and you have made a five-second question into a formula problem.

What you need

  • A Notion account — the free personal plan is enough; databases, selects, formulas, and view grouping are all included
  • About 10 minutes for the first build
  • No integrations, no bank login, no third-party tools

Step 1 — Build the Transactions database

Create a new database (table view) called Transactions. Properties:

  • Name (title) — what it was: “Groceries — Wellcome”, “Invoice #12”. Short is fine, as long as you can search for it later.
  • Date (date) — when the money moved, not when you logged it
  • Amount (number, formatted as your currency) — always enter this as a positive number, even for spending
  • Type (select) — Expense or Income. Two options. Do not add a third.
  • Category (select) — the one that does the real work; see Step 2

Entering everything as positive and letting the Type carry the sign is deliberate. If you type minus signs by hand you will forget one, and a single missing minus quietly turns a $200 expense into $400 of error in your monthly total.

Step 2 — Choose six categories, not twenty

This is the step people get wrong, and no formula fixes it afterwards. A category list is only useful if you can pick the right one in under a second at a checkout counter. Twenty options means you hesitate, then you stop logging.

Start with six:

CategoryWhat lands here
HomeRent, utilities, internet, repairs
FoodGroceries and eating out — keep them together at first
TransportFares, fuel, rides, parking
ToolsSoftware, subscriptions, hardware, anything for work
FunGoing out, hobbies, gifts, travel
OtherThe escape hatch, so you never stall on a decision

Split a category only when Other gets fat, or when one category keeps surprising you and you want to see inside it. Splitting Food into Groceries and Restaurants after two months of data is a useful decision. Doing it on day one is a guess.

Step 3 — The signed-amount formula

Add a formula property called Signed on the Transactions database:

if(prop("Type") == "Expense", prop("Amount") * -1, prop("Amount"))

That is the entire formula layer. Expenses become negative, income stays positive, and now any sum anywhere in Notion is a real net number instead of a total of two things added together that should have been subtracted.

Sanity-check it once: log one $100 income and one $40 expense. The Signed column should read 100 and -40, and a sum should show 60. If it shows 140, your Type values do not match the string in the formula. Notion compares text exactly, so expense is not Expense.

Step 4 — Two views, and only two

Views are where this becomes readable. You need exactly two on a normal month.

Inbox — the one you actually type into

  • Table view, filtered to Date is within the past week
  • Sorted by Date descending
  • Set as the default view, and put it on your Notion home page

This is the surface you touch daily. It is short, so adding a row feels cheap, which is the only reason the log stays alive.

This month — the one that answers the question

  • Table view, filtered to Date is within the past month (a relative filter, so it never needs editing)
  • Worth knowing: Notion’s “past month” means the trailing 30 days, not the calendar month. For most people that is the more useful window anyway. If you specifically need calendar months — because rent lands on the 1st — filter on an explicit date range instead and edit it monthly, or add a formula property formatDate(prop("Date"), "YYYY-MM") and group by that.
  • Group by Category
  • On the Signed column, set the group calculation to Sum

Notion shows a subtotal per group and a total at the bottom. That grouped sum is your monthly budget report — no rollup, no relation, no second database. Most Notion budget tutorials build a Months database and a relation to get this number; on a single table, grouping gives you the same answer for zero setup.

Add a third view only for a specific question you actually have: “everything in Tools this year”, “all income Q1”. Then delete it once you have the answer. Extra views go stale the same way extra categories do.

Adding savings goals (optional, 3 minutes)

If you want a goal to aim at rather than just a record, a second small database earns its keep here. This is the one place two tables beat one.

  • New database Goals with Name, Target (number), and a Transactions relation
  • On Goals, add a rollup: relation Transactions · property Amount · calculate Sum — that is “saved so far”
  • Add a formula prop("Saved") / prop("Target") and show it as a progress bar or ring

Then link the relevant transfers to the goal as you log them. Skip this entirely if you are not saving toward something named. An unused progress bar just sits there.

Where this design breaks

1. Manual entry decays, and that is the real failure mode

No bank sync means every row is typed by you. Week one is fine. Week three you are three days behind, and reconstructing three days from memory is where people quit. The fix is logging at the moment of payment, on your phone, in the Inbox view. If that is not realistic for you, an app with bank sync will serve you better, and it is worth knowing that before you spend the ten minutes.

2. Subscriptions and other recurring charges do not enter themselves

The free plan has no recurring-row automation, so the monthly ones you would most like to catch are exactly the ones nothing reminds you about. Two honest workarounds: keep a filtered view of last month’s Tools rows and duplicate them on the 1st, or set one repeating phone reminder for the same day each month. Neither is elegant. If you see self-populating subscription rows on a free workspace, something paid is doing the work.

3. Notion does not do currency conversion

The number property formats a currency symbol; it does not convert one. Mixed-currency spending needs either one row per currency with separate views, or you converting at entry time and noting the rate in the title. There is no clean answer here, so be deliberate about which currency the total is actually in.

4. It will not reconcile against your statement

Because you typed it, it will drift from your bank by a few percent — a missed coffee here, a duplicated row there. That is fine for “where did it go?”. It is not good enough for taxes or expense claims, so check which one you need before relying on the number.

Should you build it or duplicate it?

Build it yourself. Genuinely: this is a 10-minute build, and the signed-amount formula plus grouped-sum view is a pattern you will reuse across your whole workspace. It is one of the better ways to learn how Notion databases actually fit together.

FAQ

Can Notion connect to my bank account?

No. There is no native bank sync on any Notion plan. Every transaction is entered by hand, or pushed in through a third-party integration you set up and maintain yourself. Plan around manual entry.

Does this work on the free plan?

Yes. Databases, select properties, formulas, relations, rollups, and grouped view calculations are all on the free personal plan. Nothing in this guide needs a paid tier.

Do I need a separate database for each month?

No, and it is worth avoiding. One Transactions table plus a relative date filter gives you every month forever. Monthly databases mean twelve places to look and no way to compare across them.

How do I handle a refund?

Log it as Income with the original category. The signed formula then cancels the original expense inside that category’s monthly sum, which is exactly what a refund should do to your numbers.

Can I import a CSV from my bank?

Notion can import a CSV into a database, so a monthly export is a reasonable middle path if daily entry is not realistic for you. Expect to clean it: bank descriptions are unreadable, and nothing assigns your categories for you.

How long until this tells me anything useful?

One full month. A partial month grouped by category looks alarming and means nothing. You need one complete cycle including rent and the annual charges that happened to land in it before any number is worth acting on.
